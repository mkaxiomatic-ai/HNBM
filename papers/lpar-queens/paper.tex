\begin{abstract}
Combinatorial problems are increasingly solved by energy minimization, yet the encodings that make
this possible are rarely verified. We present a Lean~4 formalization of one such encoding, proved
correct in both directions. We first formalize a class of constraint problems as a sum of squares
$\lVert Ax-b\rVert^2$ --- an objective vanishing exactly on the solutions --- and prove that each is
the energy of a $\{0,1\}$ Boltzmann machine whose minimizers are precisely those solutions, with an
energy gap of at least $\tfrac12$. We then encode $n$-Queens Completion, the question whether a
partial placement of queens on a chessboard extends to a full one, and prove the encoding sound and
complete, together with the agreement of its Boolean checker with the chess condition stated
over~$\mathbb{Z}$. Completeness is what makes a negative answer meaningful: we exhibit a $7\times7$
partial placement whose two queens do not attack each other, and prove that it admits no completion.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Hopfield networks~\citeref{hopfield1982}, introduced in 1982, model associative memory: a network of
neurons with symmetric weights has an energy, asynchronous updates never increase it, and the network
therefore reaches a stable state. Boltzmann machines were introduced by Ackley, Hinton and
Sejnowski~\citeref{ackley1985} in 1985 as a stochastic generalization, in which neuron updates are
probabilistic and the network samples from a distribution over states instead of converging to a
single stable state. In our LPAR-26 paper~\citeref{hnbm} we formalized both in Lean~4, together with
their convergence and ergodicity.

That paper also records a concrete computational role for these models: minimizing a Hopfield energy
is, up to an affine change of variables, quadratic unconstrained binary optimization
(QUBO)~\citeref{lucas2014ising, glover2019}, the native input format of quantum annealers, so a
machine-checked account of the dynamics gives a specification against which the algorithms running on
such hardware could be verified. It verifies no such algorithm, however, because it verifies no
encoding. A problem must first be translated into an energy, and it is the translation that decides
whether an answer means anything: a correct solver given an unfaithful encoding returns a wrong
answer, and its failures say nothing at all. This paper supplies one translation and proves it
correct.

\secref{sec:prelim} recalls the two models, fixes a class of constraint problems whose objective
vanishes exactly on the solutions, and shows that the objective is one of their energies, so that
solving the constraints and minimizing the energy are the same task. \secref{sec:queens} formulates
an \textsf{NP}-complete problem this way, \secref{sec:correct} proves the formulation faithful, and
\secref{sec:solver}--\secref{sec:meas} run the algorithms of~\citeref{liwang2022} on it unchanged.

What is \emph{not} claimed: that the solver is fast --- a short backtracking program beats it on every
instance, and we give the numbers --- or that any algorithm here is new. What is new is that the
translation from chessboard to network is proved correct in both directions, so the solver's negative
answers mean something.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:prelim}

\subsection{Hopfield networks and Boltzmann machines}\label{sec:hnbm}

We recall the two models, and what is proved about them in~\citeref{hnbm}.

A \emph{Hopfield network} has $n$ neurons, each holding $0$ or $1$, a symmetric weight matrix $W$ with
zero diagonal, and one threshold $\theta_u$ per neuron. A state $x$ has energy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:E}
  E(x) \;=\; -\tfrac12\sum_{u \neq v} W_{uv}\,x_u x_v \;+\; \sum_u \theta_u x_u ,
\end{equation}
and the \emph{local field} at $u$ is $\sum_{v}W_{uv}x_v - \theta_u$. The network runs by repeatedly
picking a neuron and setting it to $1$ if its local field is positive and to $0$ otherwise. Such an
update can never increase $E$, and as there are finitely many states the process must reach one that
no single update changes. That is Hopfield's convergence theorem, Theorem~8.1 of~\citeref{hnbm}.

A \emph{Boltzmann machine} keeps~\eqref{eq:E} but makes the update stochastic: neuron $u$ becomes $1$
with probability $\sigma(\text{field}/T)$, for the logistic function $\sigma$ and a temperature
$T > 0$. It no longer halts but wanders, and at fixed $T$ the resulting Markov chain has a unique
stationary distribution, the Boltzmann law $\propto e^{-E(x)/T}$, which concentrates on low-energy
states as $T$ falls. Establishing that uniqueness required formalizing the Perron--Frobenius theorem,
which is \S4 of~\citeref{hnbm}.

Both are formalized there over an abstract network structure, and we use that development unchanged.
What follows adds an instance, not a theory.

\subsection{Constraint problems in canonical form}\label{sec:qubo}

Take $n$ Boolean unknowns $x_1,\dots,x_n$ and $m$ constraints, each saying that some subset of the
unknowns contains exactly one $1$. Let $A$ be the $m\times n$ matrix with $A_{r,u} = 1$ when unknown
$u$ occurs in constraint $r$, and let $b$ be the vector of $m$ ones. Constraint $r$ then says that the
$r$-th entry of $Ax$ equals that of $b$, so satisfying all of them at once is solving $Ax = b$ over
$\{0,1\}$. Rather than solve it, we measure failure:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:p}
  p(x) \;=\; \lVert Ax - b\rVert^2 \;=\; \sum_{r=1}^{m}\Bigl(\ \sum_{u \in r} x_u - b_r\ \Bigr)^{\!2}.
\end{equation}
Every summand is a squared integer, so $p \ge 0$, and $p(x) = 0$ exactly when every constraint holds;
such an $x$ is \emph{feasible}. Two easy facts are used throughout.

\begin{lemma}[integrality]\label{lem:int}
$p(x)$ is a non-negative integer, so $p(x) \neq 0$ implies $p(x) \ge 1$.
\end{lemma}

No state has a small positive objective value: a search either lands on a solution or misses by a
whole unit. \secref{sec:meas} shows this happening.

\begin{lemma}[at-most-one by one slack variable]\label{lem:slack}
For a set $S$ of unknowns and a fresh Boolean $s$, the constraint $\sum_{u\in S}x_u + s = 1$ is
satisfiable iff $\sum_{u\in S}x_u \le 1$, and then $s = 1 - \sum_{u\in S}x_u$.
\end{lemma}

This is how one says \enquote{at most one} in a language that has only \enquote{exactly one}. If
nothing in $S$ is set then $s$ takes up the slack; if one thing is, $s = 0$; if two or more are, no
value of $s$ works and the row contributes at least $1$ to~\eqref{eq:p}. A single slack suffices
however large $S$ is.

The form~\eqref{eq:p} accepts exactly the \emph{feasibility} problems whose constraints are
equalities, possibly after slack variables. In Lucas's catalogue~\citeref{lucas2014ising} exact cover
(\S4.1) needs no slack and graph colouring (\S6.1) one per edge and colour, while vertex cover (\S4.3)
and the travelling salesman (\S7.2) do not fit at all: they ask for a \emph{best} solution, and
\eqref{eq:p} cannot express a preference among feasible points. The catalogue has no $n$-queens
formulation, so the encoding below is ours rather than a transcription. In Lean the data
of~\eqref{eq:p} is a structure \lstinline{Problem} that mentions no application; graph colouring,
exact cover, Sudoku and queens are all values of it.

\subsection{The objective is an energy}\label{sec:net}

Expanding~\eqref{eq:p} gives $p(x) = x^{\top}\!A^{\top}\!Ax - 2b^{\top}\!Ax + \lVert b\rVert^2$, which
is quadratic but carries a diagonal term $(A^{\top}\!A)_{uu}x_u^2$ that~\eqref{eq:E} has no room for.
Since $x_u \in \{0,1\}$ we may replace $x_u^2$ by $x_u$, making that term linear so it can be absorbed
into the threshold; and as $A$ is a $0/1$ matrix, $(A^{\top}\!A)_{uu} = \deg(u)$, the number of
constraints containing $u$.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:net}
For any well-formed \lstinline{Problem}, setting $W_{uv} = -(A^{\top}\!A)_{uv}$ off the diagonal and
$\theta_u = \tfrac12\deg(u) - \sum_{r\ni u}b_r$ gives $E(x) = \tfrac12\bigl(p(x) - \lVert
b\rVert^2\bigr)$. Hence $E$ attains its minimum $-\tfrac12\lVert b\rVert^2$ exactly at the feasible
states, and by \cref{lem:int} every infeasible state lies at least $\tfrac12$ above it.
\end{thm}

That gap of $\tfrac12$ does not shrink as the problem grows, so on a finite state space the Boltzmann
weight of an infeasible state is smaller than a feasible one's by a factor of at least $e^{-1/2T}$;
with the uniqueness result of \secref{sec:hnbm} this gives that the chain's equilibrium distribution
concentrates on the solutions as $T \to 0^{+}$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Where the certification stops.} We identify the \emph{objective} with the
energy, and prove the \emph{integer routine} in the solver's inner loop equal to the local field. We
do \emph{not} certify the \emph{trajectory}: equations~(3) and~(6) of~\citeref{liwang2022} accumulate
their input, $u \leftarrow u + (Wx-\theta)$, which is not the single-neuron update of
\secref{sec:hnbm}, and the two agree only on the first inner iteration, when $u$ is still zero. So the
objective, the field and the minimizers are certified; the path through the state space is not.

\section{Problem formulation}\label{sec:queens}

A queen attacks along its row, its column and both diagonals. The classical $n$-queens problem asks
for $N$ mutually non-attacking queens on an $N\times N$ board, and has a closed-form solution for
every $N \ge 4$, so an equivalence about it would be true but empty. We use the completion variant: an
instance is a board size together with \emph{givens}, squares already carrying a queen, and it is a
\textsf{yes} instance when the placement extends to a full solution. This is \textsf{NP}-complete, and
counting its solutions is $\#\textsf{P}$-complete~\citeref{gent2017}.

Solvability genuinely depends on the givens and cannot be seen by inspection. A corner queen is fatal
at $N = 4$ and $N = 6$ and harmless elsewhere: the completions of $\langle n,\{(0,0)\}\rangle$ for
$n = 4,\dots,10$ number $0,2,0,4,4,28,64$. Our corpus contains a $7\times7$ board whose two givens
share no row, column or diagonal --- so nothing is locally wrong --- and which admits no completion.

Take one unknown $x_{i,j}$ per square, reading \enquote{a queen stands on $(i,j)$}.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llc@{}}
\toprule
condition & row & how many\\
\midrule
board row $i$ holds exactly one queen & $\sum_j x_{i,j}=1$ & $N$\\
column $j$ holds exactly one queen & $\sum_i x_{i,j}=1$ & $N$\\
diagonal $d$ holds at most one & $\sum_{(i,j)\in d}x_{i,j}+s_d=1$ & $2N-3$\\
anti-diagonal $a$ holds at most one & $\sum_{(i,j)\in a}x_{i,j}+s_a=1$ & $2N-3$\\
the given at $(i,j)$ is present & $x_{i,j}=1$ & one each\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The first two families are equalities already; the diagonal families are at-most-one and use
\cref{lem:slack}; a given is a row containing a single unknown, so nothing else is needed to fix
variables in advance. Only diagonals of length at least two get a row, a single square being unable
to hold two queens, and of the $2N-1$ diagonals in each direction the two extreme ones are corners.
So there are $N^2 + 2(2N-3) = N^2+4N-6$ unknowns and $6N-6+\#\text{givens}$ rows --- for the ordinary
board, $90$ and $42$.

Two remarks. The degrees are uneven: an interior square lies in four rows, each of the four corners in
three (one of its diagonals is a lone square and carries no row), and a slack in one, so
$\theta_u = -\tfrac12\deg(u)$ takes the values $-2$, $-1.5$, $-0.5$. Half-integers are harmless
for~\eqref{eq:E}, whose $\theta$ is real, but we store $\theta$ in an integer array to keep the
solver's inner loop in exact integer arithmetic. An earlier version of the library stored $\theta$
itself and kept every degree even by writing each constraint twice, which doubled the rows, the
objective and the work per sweep; storing $2\theta$ removed that. Sudoku, where every unknown has
degree four, would never have prompted it. And two queens share a diagonal when $i - a = k - b$, but
subtraction on $\mathbb{N}$ is truncated, so the test is $i + b = k + a$ instead;
\secref{sec:correct} proves the two agree.

\section{Correctness of the encoding}\label{sec:correct}

\subsection{Soundness, completeness, and the decision equivalence}

A board is an array of $N$ column indices, $q_i$ being the column of the queen in row $i$, so one
queen per row is built into the representation. \lstinline{isQueens} is the executable checker: right
length, every entry on the board, no two rows sharing a column or a diagonal, every given present.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:iff}
If every given queen lies on the board then $p(x) = 0$ implies \lstinline{isQueens (decode x)};
\lstinline{isQueens q} implies $p(\texttt{encode }q) = 0$; and therefore
$(\exists x.\,p(x)=0) \iff (\exists q.\,\texttt{isQueens }q)$.
\end{thm}

That hypothesis is the only one, and in particular there is no lower bound on $N$: the degenerate
boards are discharged rather than excluded, since at $N = 0$ there are no unknowns and no rows, $p$
is an empty sum, and the empty board is vacuously a completion. What must be excluded is a given lying
off the board, whose row would contain no unknown and could never be satisfied.

\subsection{The checker is the chess condition}

\thmref{thm:iff} is stated against \lstinline{isQueens}, a \lstinline{Bool} function we wrote, so by
itself it leaves the specification untested --- and \secref{sec:queens} has just admitted that the
diagonals are tested in rearranged form. We therefore also state the condition as a textbook would,
over $\mathbb{Z}$ with real subtraction: \lstinline{Attack i a k b} is
$a = b \vee i - a = k - b \vee i + a = k + b$, and \lstinline{IsCompletion} says the board has the
right size, every queen is on it, no two distinct rows attack, and every given is present. We prove
\lstinline{isQueens_iff}, that the checker decides exactly this, and hence
$(\exists x.\,p(x)=0) \iff (\exists q.\,\texttt{IsCompletion }q)$ --- whose right-hand side mentions
no matrix, no array and no truncated arithmetic. As a further check, \lstinline{isQueens} is compared
against an independent list-based implementation over $\mathbb{Z}$ on every board of size four and of
size five, by kernel reduction.

\subsection{Boards with no completion}

The second direction of \thmref{thm:iff} is the one worth having. Soundness could be had by checking
the answer afterwards, which is cheap; completeness cannot, since it says that had a solution existed
the objective would have had a zero, so no zero means no solution. That makes the following available,
each of the form $\neg\exists q.\,\texttt{IsCompletion }q$ --- statements about chessboards, with no
mention of the encoding.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lllc@{}}
\toprule
instance & board and givens & why blocked & size of check\\
\midrule
\texttt{attacking} & $8\times8$; $(0,0),(1,1)$ & the givens share a diagonal & none needed\\
\texttt{blocked4}  & $4\times4$; $(0,0)$ & a corner queen is fatal at $N=4$ & $4^3 = 64$\\
\texttt{blocked6}  & $6\times6$; $(0,0)$ & and at $N=6$ & $6^5 = 7776$\\
\texttt{blocked7}  & $7\times7$; $(0,0),(1,6)$ & the givens do \emph{not} attack & $7^5 = 16807$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The checker reads only the first $N$ entries, so a hypothetical completion is one of finitely many
tuples and the givens pin some coordinates. The enumeration is ordinary kernel reduction; no
\lstinline{native_decide} is used anywhere, so no compiler is in the trusted base. By \thmref{thm:iff}
each result upgrades to a claim about every bit vector --- for \texttt{blocked7}, about $2^{71}$
assignments, obtained from a check over $16807$ tuples.

\section{Neurodynamic algorithms}\label{sec:solver}

The search is Algorithm~2 of~\citeref{liwang2022}, used unchanged, and nothing in it is
queens-specific. It keeps a population of $N$ copies of a neurodynamic model, runs each until it
settles, records the best state so far as the \emph{incumbent}, re-initializes the population around
the incumbent by a particle-swarm rule, and applies random bit flips when a diversity measure shows
the copies have collapsed together; it stops after $M$ rounds without improvement. Each copy is either
the deterministic recurrence of eq.~(3), which we call DHNm, or its stochastic counterpart eq.~(6),
BMm. We implement both. Li and Wang precede the search with an Algorithm~1, a deterministic presolve
that is Sudoku constraint propagation and which on five of their ten puzzles finishes the job alone.
Queens has no such stage here; an analogue exists --- if a row or column has exactly one square not
attacked by a given, a queen must go there --- and is not implemented.

Reproducing~\citeref{liwang2022} needs values for the initial temperature, cooling rate, inner
iteration count, mutation probability, diversity threshold $\mathcal{T}$, and swarm coefficients
$c_0,c_1,c_2$; none is stated there, so all were recovered experimentally. Two mattered beyond tuning.
The pull of the incumbent, $c_2$, must be well below $c_1$, or every copy is dragged onto the
incumbent and, once that sits on a plateau, the population collapses. And $\mathcal{T}$ must be made
scale-free, since eq.~(8)'s diversity is at most $1/\sqrt{n}$ and a fixed threshold therefore means
something different on each instance; we compare against the diversity scaled by $\sqrt{n}$. The
recovered value is model-specific, $0.9$ for DHNm against $0.4$ for BMm, and having been fitted on
Sudoku it \emph{transfers}: on the empty $8\times8$ board DHNm fails at BMm's $0.4$ and succeeds on
$9$ of $10$ seeds at $0.9$.

exactly when a column or diagonal row of $A$ carries two set unknowns, so what one watches
disappearing is the residual of~\eqref{eq:p}. One widget is live rather than a replay: the reader
places queens by clicking and runs the solver on the resulting board through Lean's
remote-procedure-call mechanism, so the code that runs is the code the theorems are about. The
single-machine widgets are pinned to seeds that succeed and anneal five times more slowly than the
default; the honest summary of what one machine can do is the last column above.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\noindent\textbf{The problem itself.} Bell and Stevens survey what is known about
$n$-queens~\citeref{bell2009}. Completing a partial placement is a question of Nauck's from 1850, and
Gent, Jefferson and Nightingale settled its complexity~\citeref{gent2017}. How many queens may be
placed before completion can fail is now known fairly precisely: Glock, Munh\'{a} Correia and Sudakov
showed that any $n/60$ mutually non-attacking queens can always be completed, and that some
placements of about $n/4$ cannot~\citeref{glock2022}. Their bound is asymptotic, so it says nothing
about our $7\times7$ example. Counting solutions of the unconstrained problem is a separate
line~\citeref{simkin2021}.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Solving $n$-queens with a neural network} is not new, and is in fact done
better elsewhere. Ma\'{n}dziuk built a binary Hopfield-type network for exactly this
problem~\citeref{mandziuk1995}, and as \secref{sec:notclaim} records his succeeds where ours does
not. M\'{e}rida, Mu\~{n}oz and Ben\'{i}tez used the problem as the benchmark for a multivalued recurrent
network~\citeref{merida2001}. Both descend from Hopfield and Tank's proposal to solve optimization
problems by minimizing an energy, and from Wilson and Pawley's subsequent finding that doing so is
delicate. The solver we use belongs to the collaborative neurodynamic framework of Che and
Wang~\citeref{che2019}, which does come with a global convergence result --- but for an
augmented-Lagrangian model, not for the momentum recurrences that~\citeref{liwang2022} iterates, and
that gap is what \secref{sec:net} describes.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Encoding problems as QUBO} is a well-developed craft, with Lucas's
catalogue~\citeref{lucas2014ising} and the tutorial of Glover, Kochenberger and
Du~\citeref{glover2019} as standard references. The closest work to \secref{sec:queens} is
Codognet's~\citeref{codognet2023}, which compares several QUBO encodings of the at-most-one constraint
\emph{using $n$-queens as its example}, on a quantum annealer and on a quantum-inspired engine. That
is the same constraint on the same problem, and the difference is this paper's whole point: there the
encodings are compared by measuring how well they solve, and none is proved to be faithful.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Proving encodings correct} has been taken up for propositional formalisms, on
the observation that a trustworthy solver fed an untrustworthy encoding still yields an untrustworthy
answer. Codel, Avigad and Heule verify CNF encodings in Lean, at-most-one and at-most-$k$ among
them~\citeref{codel2023}. Szeider's PBLean brings pseudo-Boolean proof certificates into Lean~4 with
the same aim we have, formalizing the translation together with its correctness proof so that theorems
about the original problem follow~\citeref{szeider2026}. On the solver side, the VeriPB line of work
produces checkable certificates for pseudo-Boolean and constraint solving~\citeref{bogaerts2022}, and
Subercaseaux and Heule turned to a verified approach precisely because their encoding had grown too
intricate to trust~\citeref{subercaseaux2023}. What we add is the same step for the form
$\lVert Ax-b\rVert^2$ rather than for clauses or cutting planes, where the target is a network and the
objective is its energy.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Formalizing this problem} has been done once before, and instructively.
Malekpour verified in PVS an algorithm producing an $n$-queens solution for every
$n > 3$~\citeref{malekpour2020}. That verifies a \emph{construction} for the variant that always has a
solution; we verify a \emph{reduction} for the variant that is \textsf{NP}-complete, and it is the
reduction that makes a negative answer meaningful. More distantly, G\"{a}her and Kunze mechanised the
Cook--Levin theorem in Coq~\citeref{gaeher2021}, formalizing polynomial-time reductions in general
rather than one encoding for one solver. Closest in shape, and at this venue, is Weber's SAT-based
Sudoku solver~\citeref{weber2006}: a puzzle encoded into a solver formalism and evaluated
empirically, with no proof that the encoding is right. To the best of our knowledge, no QUBO or Ising
encoding has previously been proved correct in a proof assistant.

\section{Concluding remarks}\label{sec:notclaim}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item No claim of speed; see \secref{sec:meas}.
\item No new algorithm, and no improvement on the neural-network literature. Ma\'{n}dziuk's
network~\citeref{mandziuk1995} uses $N^2$ neurons and no auxiliary variables, and reports convergence of
$100\%$ (once $99\%$) for $n = 4,8,16,32,48,64,80$, with the average number of rounds fitted by
$2.82\,n^{0.67}$. On the boards where comparison is meaningful --- the empty ones --- our population
search matches that up to $N = 8$ and then falls away: we fail at $N = 10$ where he succeeds at
$N = 80$. Three reasons, none in our favour. His update descends the energy at every step and so
converges, whereas the recurrences we inherit accumulate and do not (\secref{sec:net}). His encoding
is built for the problem and needs $N^2$ neurons, where a generic reduction costs us $N^2+4N-6$, the
excess being slacks that carry no information but must still be got right. And our constants were
recovered on Sudoku, whose pipeline has a presolve stage that queens lacks. A reader wanting an
effective neurodynamic $n$-queens solver should use his.
\item The search is a heuristic and nothing proves it succeeds. What is proved is that its positive
answers are correct, and that its objective has a zero exactly when the board can be completed.
\item For the backtracking baseline we prove soundness only.
\item Nothing about cooling schedules. \secref{sec:net} concerns the equilibrium distribution at a
fixed temperature, in the limit as that temperature falls; it does not say how slowly a real run must
cool to stay near equilibrium. This matters because our single-machine results are poor: the theorem
says the difficulty lies in the schedule rather than in the encoding, and it is not a remedy.
\item Nothing about the accumulating recurrences of eqs.~(3) and~(6).
\end{itemize}

In summary, $n$-Queens Completion is formulated here as a quadratic unconstrained binary optimization
problem; that formulation is proved faithful in both directions, and against a specification of the
chess condition rather than against a checker we wrote; and the objective it defines is proved to be
the energy of a $\{0,1\}$ Boltzmann machine of~\citeref{hnbm} whose minimizers are exactly the
completions. The algorithms of~\citeref{liwang2022} then run on it unchanged. What the proofs buy is
not speed but meaning: a run that ends without a zero has established something about the chessboard.

The natural next step is to certify the trajectory and not only the objective. Nothing is known to us
about the accumulating recurrences of eqs.~(3) and~(6), whereas the memoryless single-neuron chain is
already covered by~\citeref{hnbm}; a solver restricted to that chain would therefore be certified from
the chessboard to the last update, which neither this paper nor its predecessor yet achieves.
